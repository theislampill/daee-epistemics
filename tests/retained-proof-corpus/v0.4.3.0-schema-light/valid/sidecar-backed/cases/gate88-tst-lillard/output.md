**Field Read**

The statement is not merely an emotional dislike of punishment. It is a layered moral, epistemic, theological, and source-authority objection:

“I would never worship a God who punishes non-belief eternally, while knowing what would convince every person; such a God would be cruel, inhumane, unkind, ungenerous, and unworthy of worship.”

The first duty is to separate the burdens before answering. If they remain fused, the discussion becomes a single emotional verdict: hell feels cruel, therefore the God who warns of hell is unworthy. That compression hides several prior questions: what counts as proof, what counts as culpable rejection, whether divine guidance must equal forced or guaranteed uptake, whether mercy and justice can coexist with punishment, and what authority is being used to judge God.

No judgment is made here about Richard-Lael Lillard’s inward sincerity, final fate, or soul-state. The public identity labels attached to him may explain the worldview carrier being engaged, but they are not proof of motive, guilt, or spiritual condition. The statement itself is enough to analyze.

**Noetic Frame**

Selected field: mixed moral tribunal, hiddenness, accountability, source-worldview objection.

The live pressure is mixed because the argument moves across five registers at once:

- heart: moral recoil, worship refusal, cruelty language, and affective disgust.
- ξ: source demand, warrant standard, proof criterion, and “what would convince” logic.
- Ω: divine predicates such as kindness, generosity, justice, cruelty, and worship-worthiness.
- μ: worldview-carrier pressure from Satanist/TST identity and the request to dismantle a belief system.
- κ: dependency pressure between unbelief, evidence, culpability, punishment, mercy, and divine knowledge.

The response must therefore avoid two failures. It must not answer with cold abstraction that ignores the moral wound in the objection, and it must not concede the hidden premise that human moral recoil can sit above God as the final court without first accounting for its own source and authority.

**State Surface**

Δκ live: the statement depends on several hidden transitions.

∇·κ: positive/live, because the objection has real moral force only if its premises are granted: that non-belief is always innocent non-conviction; that God’s knowledge of convincing conditions creates an obligation to produce guaranteed belief; that eternal punishment is detached from justice, warning, rejection, and culpability; and that the speaker’s moral standard has independent authority over the divine.

∇×κ: unresolved until the moral tribunal is repaired. The argument condemns God by appealing to moral realities such as cruelty, kindness, generosity, and justice, but then must explain where those moral realities come from, why they bind, and why they outrank the One being judged. If the moral language is only preference, revulsion, social sentiment, or self-authored value, it cannot carry the weight of “not worthy of worship.” If it is objective, then the objection must account for objective moral authority.

R(H,Δ): proceed by separating the burdens, not by attacking the person.

**Burden Map**

¹B: Imported moral tribunal and worship-worthiness criterion.

The claim assumes a court of moral judgment above God: “a deity who does this is cruel and unworthy.” That criterion cannot be smuggled in as obvious. It must be grounded. In Islamic terms, the Creator is not judged by a moral law external to Him as though He were one citizen inside a larger order. Rather, Allah is al-Ḥakam, al-ʿAdl, al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm: the source of judgment, justice, mercy, and right measure. Human moral perception matters, but it is not sovereign over revelation.

²B: Accountability and hujjah compression.

“Simple fact of non-belief” compresses too much. Islamic accountability is not a crude rule that every person who lacks belief at any moment is punished identically. The Qur’an distinguishes hearing, understanding, rejection, arrogance, concealment, excuse, incapacity, and proof being established. “We never punish until We have sent a messenger” is a central accountability principle, not a footnote. The objection must not collapse non-exposure, confusion, trauma, misrepresentation, negligence, sincere searching, stubborn rejection, and hostile concealment into one category.

³B: Hiddenness and coercive-guidance demand.

“God knows what would convince everyone” is true at the level of divine knowledge, but the conclusion does not follow. Knowing what would compel or guarantee belief does not mean justice requires coercive disclosure, irresistible evidence, or removal of moral agency. The Islamic account distinguishes signs, reminders, messengers, fitrah, reason, and revelation from forced submission of the heart. A test in which refusal, humility, pride, repentance, and sincerity are meaningful cannot be reduced to “God should produce whatever stimulus guarantees assent.”

⁴B: Punishment proportionality with mercy-justice architecture.

The hell objection must separate punishment imagery, duration, culpability, warning, divine justice, divine mercy, and the ontology of rebellion against God. Islam does not present punishment as divine sadism. It presents punishment as just recompense after warning, proof, denial, corruption, arrogance, and refusal, while also presenting Allah’s mercy as vast, His forgiveness as open, and His judgment as perfectly knowing. The objection quotes the emotional weight of punishment but often omits the prior moral history of the punished subject and the divine knowledge of what each person actually knew, rejected, loved, concealed, or pursued.

⁵B: Source-worldview and identity-stabilization burden.

The TST/Satanist frame should be handled as an authority-order question, not as a slur. Modern Satanic Temple discourse often frames Satan symbolically as rebellion against arbitrary authority, religious coercion, and oppressive dogma. That gives the objection a recognizable structure: autonomy is treated as morally safer than submission, and divine command is suspected as tyranny unless it passes a human rights tribunal. The response should therefore ask: what is the final source of moral obligation in that worldview, and can it sustain the condemnation it makes?

**Opening Answer**

The statement should be dealt with by first refusing its compression. It says “simple non-belief,” but Islamic accountability is not built on a simplistic non-belief switch. It says “God knows what would convince everyone,” but divine knowledge does not imply an obligation to override agency or make rejection impossible. It says “cruel, inhumane, not kind, not generous,” but those judgments require a moral standard with authority. It says “not worthy of worship,” but worship-worthiness cannot be measured by whether the Creator conforms to a creature’s preferred terms of governance.

The correct opening move is therefore not to begin with “hell is real, accept it” in a flat way, nor to dilute revelation to make it emotionally acceptable. The correct move is to expose the architecture of the claim:

1. If moral condemnation is objective, where does its authority come from?
2. If moral condemnation is subjective, why should it bind God or anyone else?
3. If non-belief can be innocent, culpable, negligent, arrogant, confused, inherited, wounded, or defiant, why call all of it “simple”?
4. If God knows what would convince every person, why assume justice requires guaranteed uptake rather than sufficient signs, messengers, fitrah, reason, warning, and accountable response?
5. If punishment is being judged, why isolate punishment from warning, mercy, repentance, proof, rejection, and divine knowledge of the whole person?
6. If TST-style Satanic autonomy is the alternate worldview, what grounds its moral outrage beyond preference, social contract, or selective inheritance from theistic moral capital?

A governed answer must keep the human tone clear: the objection is emotionally understandable. Many people hear descriptions of hell and feel horror before they have any disciplined account of justice, culpability, mercy, or divine right. That reaction should not be mocked. But emotional intensity is not the same as a completed argument. A person may be morally disturbed by a doctrine and still have unexamined premises controlling the disturbance.

The Islamic response begins with Allah’s own self-disclosure: He is not arbitrary cruelty. He is the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful, the Just, the Wise, the One who wrongs no one, the One who sends messengers, the One who accepts repentance, the One whose mercy encompasses all things, and the One whose judgment is not limited by our partial knowledge. The Qur’an repeatedly denies that Allah wrongs people, while also warning that rejection after truth, arrogance before signs, corruption, and denial are morally serious.

So the first dismantling is not a personal attack on a Satanist. It is a burden repair: the objection borrows moral seriousness while resisting the source that can ground moral seriousness; it treats disbelief as a simple innocent state while ignoring the Qur’anic distinctions of hujjah and culpability; it treats divine guidance as though only coercive certainty would be fair; and it treats punishment as cruelty while separating it from justice, warning, mercy, and the full reality of the creature’s response to God.

Terminal opening state: ¹B through ⁵B are live and bounded for execution. The answer should proceed by source-order repair, accountability clarification, hiddenness repair, punishment-proportionality calibration, attribute precision, and worldview source audit, while withholding any claim about the named individual’s inner state or final destiny.

Allah is not one moral agent among others inside a court that precedes Him. He is the Creator of persons, the giver of moral knowledge, the knower of all circumstances, the judge who knows the hidden and the apparent, and the One whose judgment is not limited by partial human access. The Qur’an repeatedly refuses the idea that God wrongs people: “Allah does not wrong the people at all, but it is the people who wrong themselves” (Qur’an 10:44). It also states, “Your Lord wrongs no one” (Qur’an 18:49). These are not decorative citations; they define the authority order. Divine punishment cannot be assessed as if God were an ignorant tyrant with partial evidence, because the revealed claim is that He judges with complete knowledge, perfect justice, and no oppression.

This does not mean every human emotional question is silenced. It means the question must be put in the right court. A sincere question may ask: How does divine justice relate to warning, proof, culpability, rejection, mercy, and individual capacity? That is a real question. But the quoted assertion does more than ask. It announces a verdict: cruel, inhumane, not kind, not generous, not worthy. Authority-order-repair therefore separates the humanly understandable pain of the question from the unauthorized sovereignty of the verdict.

The repair also protects worship from being reduced to moral consumer approval. Worship in Islam is not admiration for a deity who passes a preference test. Worship is the owed response to the true Lord: the One who creates, sustains, owns, commands, judges, forgives, guides, warns, and returns creation to account. If God is truly God, His worthiness is not created by human approval. Human approval may be healed, educated, humbled, or clarified; it does not make Him worthy. The Qur’anic order is therefore not “I approve, therefore He is worthy,” but “He is Lord, therefore my approval must be disciplined by truth, knowledge, humility, and revealed guidance.”

This matters especially in the phrase “not a God worthy of worship.” That phrase appears to make worship-worthiness depend on whether divine governance satisfies the speaker’s prior moral filter. But the Islamic concept of worship-worthiness begins with lordship and exclusive divinity, not with a finite creature’s access to the whole moral field. The creature sees a fragment: a statement about punishment, an image of fire, a concern about disbelief, and a claim about divine knowledge. God, in the Islamic account, knows the whole person, the proof that reached them, the rejection or confusion in them, the excuses they had, the arrogance or sincerity they carried, the warnings they received, the mercy they were offered, and the justice of the final ruling. Authority-order-repair does not solve every downstream question here; it prevents the downstream question from being tried in the wrong court.

A useful way to state the repaired order is this: moral concern is admissible, but it is not sovereign; emotional recoil is understandable, but it is not final evidence; human compassion is real, but it is not more knowledgeable than God; outrage may identify a pressure point, but it cannot become revelation; and worship-worthiness is not assigned by the creature to the Creator. Once that order is restored, the objection must stop presenting itself as a completed refutation and must become a set of narrower burdens: what counts as proof, what counts as culpable rejection, how justice and mercy relate, and whether the critic’s alternative worldview can ground the moral court it invokes.

The operation therefore changes the burden-local state. The statement can no longer simply say “this is cruel” as though cruelty has already been established by an independent authority. It must show why its moral tribunal is legitimate, why that tribunal outranks revealed divine self-disclosure, why finite human access is sufficient to condemn final judgment, and why worship-worthiness can be made conditional on creaturely approval. Until those are shown, the moral verdict is not dismantling God; it is exposing an ungrounded authority claim inside the objection.

Qur’an 39:53 opens the door of repentance and commands servants not to despair of God’s mercy. Qur’an 7:156 states that God’s mercy encompasses all things while also naming moral seriousness. That distinction matters. In Islamic terms, guidance is not a single flat event. There is guidance as clarification, direction, warning, and evidence; and there is guidance as the inward success and acceptance that belongs to Allah’s decree and a person’s lived response. The Qur’an can speak of the Prophet guiding in the sense of showing the path, while also saying that he does not control who receives inward guidance: “You do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills” (Qur’an 28:56). It also says the Messenger’s duty is clear conveyance (for example, Qur’an 16:82 and 24:54). Those two strands prevent both extremes: Islam does not say human beings are abandoned without signs, and it does not say the messenger or the audience controls final inward guidance as a mechanical product.

The objection becomes too quick when it treats divine knowledge of possible convincing conditions as a moral obligation to use the most coercive or irresistible condition in every case. Knowledge of what would overpower, overwhelm, or secure outward assent is not the same thing as the wisdom of making that form of persuasion the rule of moral life. A teacher may know how to make a student parrot an answer; that is not the same as cultivating understanding. A judge may know what threat would make a defendant confess; that is not the same as justice. A parent may know what pressure would make a child comply; that is not the same as forming character. The analogy is limited because God is not a creature, but the moral distinction is clear: ability to force a response does not by itself create an obligation to force it.

The Qur’anic pattern repeatedly refuses the idea that guidance must become coercion before God can be just. “There is no compulsion in religion” (Qur’an 2:256) is not a slogan for indifference; it means the truth is distinguished from error without making faith a mere product of force. The Qur’an also says, “Had your Lord willed, those on earth would have believed, all of them entirely. Then would you compel the people until they become believers?” (Qur’an 10:99). That verse directly touches the pressure in this burden: God’s power to make universal belief occur does not mean the prophetic task is to compel belief or that non-compulsion is cruelty. The objection assumes the opposite without arguing for it.

This does not remove the emotional seriousness of the question. The fear is not merely “Why did God not give more information?” It is “How could a merciful God let a person be lost?” That is a real human anguish, and it should not be answered with cold triumph. But anguish is not the same as a valid moral tribunal over God. Islam answers by holding multiple truths together: God is not unjust to anyone (Qur’an 4:40); God does not punish until a messenger has been sent in the relevant sense of establishing accountability (Qur’an 17:15); God knows inner and outer realities that human observers do not know; and no human speaker is licensed to decide the final fate of a named individual from a public label or one quoted statement. This means the objection’s broad rule, “non-belief as such gets eternal burning,” is not a careful account of hujjah, culpability, rejection, excuse, and judgment.

The operation also separates persuasion from entitlement. A person may say, “I would believe if God appeared in a form I specify, answered my chosen test, removed every ambiguity I experience, and made the matter undeniable to me.” But the moment the creature dictates the conditions under which the Creator is allowed to be worthy, the authority order has shifted. The question is no longer only about evidence; it is about who gets to set the court, the burden of proof, the moral standard, and the meaning of mercy. Islamic da'wah can meet honest questions, bring proofs, and clarify misconceptions, but it does not concede that a human demand for a particular kind of certainty has authority over Allah.

There is also a difference between “I am not convinced” and “God has failed morally.” The first can be a confession of one’s present state, and it may deserve patient clarification. The second is a theological accusation that requires a moral foundation. If the speaker uses moral terms such as cruel, inhumane, unkind, ungenerous, and unworthy, the operation asks where those terms get their binding authority. In Islam, mercy, justice, wisdom, and worship-worthiness are not measured by private recoil alone. They are grounded in Allah’s reality, names, acts, revelation, and perfect knowledge. A human being may recoil from a doctrine as understood, but the recoil itself does not establish the doctrine’s falsity or God’s unworthiness.

The hiddenness pressure also depends on a simplified picture of evidence. The Qur’an does not present guidance as one isolated proof dropped into a neutral mind. It speaks of signs in creation, signs in the self, prophetic warning, moral recognition, gratitude, arrogance, heedlessness, social imitation, desire, fear, and the covering effects of repeated rejection. That does not allow us to accuse a particular person’s soul. It does mean disbelief is not automatically a clean epistemic accident called “simple non-belief.” The heart, desires, social identity, authority loyalties, and moral posture can shape how evidence is received. A governed response therefore avoids two errors: it does not pretend every skeptic is insincere, and it does not let skepticism define itself as pure neutrality without examination.

A careful Islamic answer also refuses to turn hell into an isolated image detached from mercy and warning. The Qur’an warns of severe punishment, but those warnings sit beside repeated calls to repentance, descriptions of Allah as Most Merciful, and declarations that people are not wronged. Hell is not introduced as divine sadism; it is tied to culpable rejection, rebellion, arrogance, denial, injustice, and the consequences of turning away after truth is made binding. Whether a particular person has received that binding proof in the fullest accountable way is not ours to declare from a quote. That boundary is part of the answer: Allah’s judgment is more exact than our polemics.

The coercive-guidance demand is therefore bounded by several public checks. First, divine ability does not equal moral obligation to compel. Second, clarity and compulsion are not the same. Third, outward non-belief does not by itself exhaust the categories of exposure, understanding, rejection, excuse, and culpability. Fourth, universal forced conviction would not be the same moral world as accountable worship. Fifth, human moral recoil cannot function as the final standard over Allah unless its own authority is grounded. Sixth, Islamic sources already distinguish warning, conveyance, inward guidance, justice, mercy, and final judgment.

This submove does not claim that every reader will feel emotionally satisfied. It claims a narrower governed result: the argument “God knows what would convince everyone, therefore non-universal conviction proves cruelty” does not land as stated. It relies on a hidden rule that only irresistible guidance would be fair. Once that rule is exposed, the burden changes. The live issue becomes whether Allah has disclosed enough for accountability, judges without injustice, distinguishes culpable rejection from excused ignorance, and remains wise in allowing a non-coerced moral field. Those are serious questions, but they are not the same as the original accusation.

For da'wah practice, the answer should be firm without being contemptuous. One can say: “I understand why the image of punishment horrifies you. Islam does not ask us to be casual about hell. But your statement assumes that if God can make everyone believe, He must do so in the exact way that guarantees acceptance. That would turn guidance into compulsion and would bypass the moral reality Islam says this life tests. Islam also does not teach that we can look at a person, see the word ‘non-believer,’ and mechanically assign eternal punishment without regard to proof, rejection, excuse, and Allah’s perfect justice. This also changes how sources should be used. Qur’an 10:99 answers the universal-compulsion premise. Qur’an 2:256 blocks forced religion as the model of faith. Qur’an 28:56 distinguishes prophetic presentation from Allah’s inward guidance. Qur’an 17:15 blocks punishment without established accountability. Qur’an 4:40 blocks the claim that Allah wrongs people. Qur’an 18:29 preserves the seriousness of human response after truth is presented. Qur’an 41:53 points to signs in horizons and selves. These are not decorative citations; they map the burden-local pressure and show why the objection cannot compress guidance, conviction, accountability, and punishment into one accusation.

The same governed posture should be maintained toward the named worldview carrier. If the speaker identifies with TST or Satanism, that may explain some authority preferences or rhetorical posture, but it is not proof of motive, sincerity, or final state. The hiddenness argument must be answered as an argument. The person must be addressed as morally responsible but not reduced to a label. That prevents the response from becoming an ad hominem and keeps the focus on the pressure actually raised: whether divine knowledge of convincing conditions entails an obligation to guarantee every person’s belief.

Restorative Response
Restored criterion/order: fitrah/tawhid and sound reason/ʿaql are restored as the governing criterion, so created moral recoil is heard but not enthroned above Allah as the final judge of divine justice, mercy, warning, accountability, and worship-worthiness.
Relieved pressure: the objection is relieved from the claim that non-belief by itself, identity labels, or lack of guaranteed persuasion automatically proves divine cruelty, inhumanity, ungenerosity, or unworthiness.
Held/scoped/reopenable remainder: no generated B_MRP pressure is live; the matter remains reopenable only if a new unresolved burden is introduced, such as a specific claim about hujjah not reaching someone, coercive guidance being morally required, punishment ontology being misdescribed, or a source-worldview premise being asserted without warrant.

The first repair is to answer the pain without accepting the hidden authority order inside the objection. A person may feel horror at the thought of punishment, and that recoil should not be mocked. Islam does not ask the caller to become cold toward fear, grief, or moral confusion. But the statement does more than express pain. It quietly sets a human moral tribunal over Allah, then declares that Allah would be worthy only if He satisfies that tribunal’s prior definition of kindness and generosity.

The Islamic answer begins by restoring order: Allah is not measured by a creature’s independent court. He is the source of justice, mercy, wisdom, ownership, life, death, accountability, and return. The Qur’an repeatedly joins divine mercy with judgment rather than allowing either to cancel the other: Allah is “the Most Merciful of the merciful” while also warning of real accountability. The moral field is not “mercy or justice,” but mercy, warning, proof, patience, repentance, excuse, justice, and final judgment under the Lord who knows what we do not know.

So the response should not be, “Yes, that sounds cruel, but we accept it anyway.” It should be: the cruelty charge depends on a compressed picture. It treats disbelief as a bare mental state, assumes every person’s evidentiary history is identical, assumes persuasion must mean guaranteed uptake, assumes punishment is arbitrary harm rather than just recompense after proof and rebellion, and assumes the created moral sense can condemn its own Maker without borrowing from Him.

The phrase “simple fact of non-belief” must be narrowed. In Islamic theology, accountability is not a crude rule that every person who lacks a label is automatically judged the same way regardless of exposure, understanding, coercion, incapacity, distortion, sincerity, arrogance, or rejection after proof. The Qur’an says Allah does not punish until a messenger has been sent. It also says no soul bears another’s burden. It says Allah does not wrong anyone even by the weight of an atom. Those are not decorative verses. They stop us from speaking as if we know the fate of every named individual or every person under a sociological label.

That means the caller should refuse two errors at once. Do not soften revelation by denying Hell, warning, or accountability. Also do not speak as if you personally assign final destinations to individuals whose inner proof-history you do not know. The proper distinction is between the truth of divine warning and the hidden details of individual judgment. Hell is real. Accountability is real. Rejection after truth is grave. But Allah’s judgment is not blind bureaucracy; it is perfect knowledge, perfect justice, and no ظلم.

The hiddenness claim also needs repair. “God knows what would convince everyone” can mean several different things. If it means Allah has complete knowledge, Islam agrees. If it means Allah must force every person into willing faith by whatever would psychologically compel them, Islam does not accept that premise. Guidance in the Qur’an is not only data transfer. It includes signs, fitrah, messengers, scripture, moral testing, humility, response, turning, and Allah’s enabling. A demand for irresistible conviction can become a demand that the test be removed while still calling the result worship.

The Qur’an presents signs as abundant, not coercive. It also describes people who reject after signs because of pride, desire, social cost, envy, or refusal. This matters because “convince” can be ambiguous. A person may be given enough truth to be accountable without being forced into submission. If someone demands a mode of disclosure that leaves no room for humility, trust, repentance, love, or moral response, then the demand is not merely for evidence; it may be a demand for compulsion dressed as fairness.

That does not license accusing the objector’s soul. The correct move is conditional and principled: if someone has not received the truth in a way that establishes hujjah, Allah knows that and judges justly; if someone has received truth and rejects it because they prefer autonomy over submission, Allah knows that too. The caller is not the unseen judge. The caller clarifies the categories and invites the person back to Allah.

The punishment objection must also be decompressed. “Burned forever” is being used to make emotional immediacy do all the philosophical work. Islamic sources do describe severe punishment, and we should not sanitize what revelation warns about. But the moral inference “severe punishment therefore cruelty” does not follow unless several premises are already granted: that the offense is small, that the punished person is innocent or merely uninformed, that the judge lacks ownership, that warning was absent, that repentance was unavailable, that persistence in rebellion has no weight, and that divine rights are not real.

In tawhid, shirk and rejection of Allah are not minor paperwork failures. They are disorder at the root: denying, rivaling, or refusing the One from whom existence, moral value, mercy, reason, and every good are received. That does not mean every confused person is treated as a deliberate rebel. It means the object of the sin is not small. A finite creature can commit a qualitatively grave wrong by knowingly rejecting the infinite Lord, just as the seriousness of treason is not measured merely by the seconds it takes to utter the treasonous sentence.

Mercy is not absent from this structure. The Qur’an opens its chapters with divine mercy. It calls people to repentance. It says not to despair of Allah’s mercy. It describes Allah forgiving sins for whom He wills, accepting repentance, multiplying good, and not wronging anyone. The Prophet Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, taught Allah’s mercy in language that makes divine mercy greater than the mercy of a mother for her child. But mercy does not mean the abolition of judgment. A world where no final justice exists for arrogant evil is not more humane; it is morally broken.

The “not kind” and “not generous” predicates therefore need precision. Kindness is not the same as unconditional affirmation. Generosity is not the same as removing consequence. Mercy is not the same as denying the moral order. A judge who warns, delays, sends messengers, opens repentance, forgives abundantly, distinguishes the excused from the culpable, and judges with full knowledge is not made cruel by punishing those who knowingly persist in rejection and evil. The objector may still dislike the doctrine, but the accusation has not been established.

The TST or Satanist identity should be handled as a worldview carrier, not as a shortcut to condemnation. It may tell us something about likely authority commitments: autonomy, self-sovereignty, anti-theistic protest, symbolic rebellion, secular moral framing, or rejection of religious authority. But it does not let us infer sincerity, trauma, knowledge, culpability, or final standing before Allah. A Muslim response should not attack the person’s sexuality or identity label as if that dismantles the argument. The argument is answered by exposing its premises and restoring the source of moral authority.

The source order is central. If the person says “that is cruel,” ask: by what standard? If the answer is personal disgust, then the statement reports an emotional reaction, not a binding moral verdict on God. If the answer is human flourishing, ask why human flourishing has ultimate authority in a godless frame. If the answer is compassion, ask whether compassion is objective, why it binds reality, and why it should outrank divine justice. If the answer is autonomy, then the objection has likely revealed its root: God is rejected because He judges the self, not because the self has successfully judged God.

This is where fitrah and sound reason meet. The fitrah recognizes that cruelty, injustice, arrogance, and oppression are wrong. Islam does not erase that recognition; it purifies it. Sound reason/ʿaql then asks whether the moral recognition can stand if severed from Allah. If moral outrage is real and not merely a preference, then it points beyond the self to a moral order. But if it points beyond the self, it cannot coherently be used to dethrone the Lord who grounds that order. The objection borrows moral seriousness from a reality closer to tawhid than to Satanic self-sovereignty.

A careful answer can therefore say: your moral recoil is not worthless. It may be a sign that you still know injustice is real. But the conclusion has outrun the warrant. You have assumed that Allah punishes innocent non-persuasion, that He owes coercive proof, that no one rejects after adequate warning, that divine rights are morally negligible, and that your present moral frame is competent to judge the unseen totality of every soul’s proof, response, and final judgment. Islam denies those assumptions.

From the Qur’an, the repair rests on several stable anchors: Allah does not wrong anyone; Allah does not punish without establishing proof; every soul is judged individually; Allah’s mercy is vast; repentance remains open before death and before the final signs; warnings are real; rejection after truth is grave; and final judgment belongs to Allah alone. These anchors dismantle the flattened claim without pretending the topic is emotionally light.

From the Sunnah, the same balance appears: divine mercy is immense, but accountability is not fictional. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, warned, invited, wept, prayed for guidance, and did not treat people as abstractions. That Prophetic pattern matters. We do not answer a wounded objection with contempt. We answer with clarity, mercy, and refusal to let a false premise govern the whole field.

A useful direct response would be:

You are objecting to a version of judgment where God knowingly withholds what an innocent person would need, then punishes that person merely for failing to be persuaded. Islam does not require me to defend that version. Allah knows every person’s exposure, capacity, sincerity, distortion, arrogance, rejection, and excuse. He does not wrong anyone. He warns, sends guidance, opens repentance, and judges with perfect knowledge. What Islam does require me to defend is that the Creator has the right to be worshiped, that rejecting Him after truth is not a trivial matter, and that mercy does not erase justice.

Then add:

If your claim is that no one should be punished unless proof has been established, Islam already affirms that in a stronger form than you do, because Allah knows the inner and outer facts perfectly. If your claim is that God must guarantee that every person accepts, then you are no longer asking for justice; you are asking for the moral test to be replaced by compelled certainty. And if your claim is that your moral outrage makes God unworthy of worship, then you need to ground that moral standard without secretly borrowing from the very Lord you reject.

This response preserves daʿwah adab because it does not reduce the person to “Gay Satanist.” It can mention public worldview commitments only when relevant: The Satanic Temple style of religion often treats Satan as a symbol of rebellion against arbitrary authority rather than as an object of traditional devil-worship. That distinction matters for accuracy. Yet even symbolic Satanism still tends to place individual sovereignty and secular moral autonomy at the center. Islam’s critique is not that every adherent has identical motives; it is that autonomy cannot be the final judge of the Lord of all worlds.

The caller should also avoid overclaiming “dismantle his belief system” as if one reply guarantees collapse. The governed aim is to remove the false supports: the imported tribunal, the compressed accountability claim, the coercive hiddenness demand, the unexamined punishment premise, and the identity-carrier shortcut. Once those supports are removed, what remains is an invitation: return moral outrage to its proper source, let fitrah be corrected by revelation, and let ʿaql serve tawhid rather than prosecute it.

A concise source-backed frame can be stated this way: The Qur’an denies divine injustice, denies inherited guilt, affirms individual accountability, affirms warning before punishment, affirms vast mercy, and affirms real Hell. Therefore the Islamic doctrine is not “God burns people for the simple fact of not being convinced.” It is that Allah creates with purpose, guides through signs and messengers, knows every soul completely, excuses whom He excuses by justice and mercy, and punishes culpable rejection and evil with perfect right. The objector’s sentence attacks a caricature unless it first proves that Islamic judgment is arbitrary, uninformed, proofless, and disproportionate.

The emotional register should be restored, not crushed. A Muslim can say: I understand why the image of Hell frightens you. It frightens believers too. The Qur’an’s warnings are meant to awaken, not entertain cruelty. But fear of judgment should lead us to seek mercy, not to declare the Judge immoral. If the house is burning, the one who warns you is not cruel for describing fire. If the Lord warns repeatedly, opens the door of repentance, and judges no one unjustly, then the warning is mercy before it is punishment.

The accountability register is restored by refusing to speak beyond knowledge. We do not say this named person is certainly doomed. We do not say his labels prove his fate. We do not say every non-Muslim has the same standing. We say the claim as stated is false because it collapses categories Islam keeps separate. The burden is on the critic to show that Allah punishes someone who had no established proof, no capacity, no meaningful warning, no culpable rejection, and no just basis for accountability. Islamic sources do not grant that picture.

The hiddenness register is restored by distinguishing sufficient guidance from forced submission. Allah could create a world in which no one disobeys, but that is not the world of moral trial described by revelation. The existence of a test is not cruelty. A test becomes unjust if the examiner is ignorant, biased, deceptive, or punishes without proof. Those defects do not apply to Allah. He knows fully, sends guidance, gives capacity, accounts for circumstance, and judges with perfect justice.

The punishment register is restored by distinguishing sentimental mercy from holy mercy. Sentimental mercy says no serious consequence may remain if consequence makes me uncomfortable. Holy mercy says the door is opened, warnings are sent, repentance is accepted, good is multiplied, evil is not exaggerated, and final justice is not abolished. The Qur’anic Lord is not a projection of therapeutic approval. He is al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm, al-ʿAdl in meaning, al-Ḥakīm, al-ʿAlīm, al-Malik.

The source-worldview register is restored by testing authority. If someone follows a Satanic or secular autonomy frame, the question is not merely “what do you feel about Hell?” The question is: what has the right to define good, evil, personhood, guilt, justice, mercy, and worship? If the answer is the autonomous self or the current moral consensus, then the objection has no stable throne. Consensus changes. Feelings conflict. Power edits moral fashion. The self can condemn God today and excuse itself tomorrow. Tawhid gives moral language a real source, even when revelation corrects our first reactions.

The final daʿwah movement should be invitational: the part of you that hates cruelty is not your enemy. But let it be disciplined by truth. Do not let a real moral instinct be captured by a false picture of Allah. The One who created you knows what would heal you, what would expose you, what would overwhelm you, what you have seen, what you have refused, what wounded you, and what you chose. He is not less merciful than you. He is not less just than you. Your mercy is a created trace; His mercy is the source. Your reason is a gift; His knowledge surrounds all things.

That is the restored endpoint: worship-worthiness belongs to Allah because He is Allah, not because He passes an imported tribunal. Human moral concern is honored when it returns to fitrah/tawhid, not when it becomes a weapon against the Lord. Sound reason/ʿaql does not require denying Hell, denying warning, or denying judgment. It requires refusing caricature, refusing category collapse, refusing identity-based condemnation, and refusing to call perfect justice cruel merely because created autonomy does not want to be judged.

The remaining pressure can be held without expanding the claim beyond evidence. If the person raises a concrete case, such as someone who never encountered Islam except through abuse, propaganda, incapacity, or severe distortion, the answer is not to flatten that person into the same category as a willful rejecter. The answer is that Allah knows the exact proof-history and judges it without ظلم. That scoped humility is not retreat from doctrine; it is obedience to the doctrine that final judgment belongs to Allah with complete knowledge.

The restored response therefore keeps three boundaries clear. It does not deny the reality of Hell. It does not presume the final fate of a named individual from an identity label. It does not allow created outrage to become the criterion over revelation. Within those boundaries, the caller can be emotionally honest and theologically firm: fear of punishment is understandable, but the charge of cruelty requires premises Islam does not grant.

So the invitation remains: bring the moral concern back under the Lord who grounds mercy and justice. Ask whether the objection is really against injustice, which Islam rejects, or against being accountable to Allah at all. If it is the first, tawhid answers it with perfect judgment and no wrongdoing. If it is the second, the issue is no longer divine cruelty; it is the self resisting worship, warning, and return.
Closing Formulation

### Established failure

The assertion fails as a governing objection because it tries to make moral recoil do more work than it can lawfully do. The recoil is intelligible: a person hears “eternal punishment,” imagines arbitrary burning for mere non-assent, and reacts with horror. That emotional pressure should not be mocked or bypassed. But the statement then converts that recoil into a verdict: therefore the Deity is cruel, inhumane, not kind, not generous, and unworthy of worship. That conversion is where the argument breaks.

The first failure is the imported tribunal. The claim assumes a moral court above God by which God is measured, but it does not identify where that court comes from, why it is binding, or why its verdict is more authoritative than revelation. If the speaker means only “I personally dislike this,” then the conclusion cannot reach “God is unworthy.” If the speaker means “this is objectively unjust,” then the speaker has already invoked a moral reality that must be grounded. In Islamic terms, sound moral recognition is not denied; fiṭrah and ʿaql ṣarīḥ can recognize cruelty, injustice, mercy, generosity, and moral disorder. But fiṭrah and sound reason do not become an independent throne over Allah. They are created capacities that must be disciplined by tawḥīd, revelation, and truthful judgment.

The second failure is compression. “The simple fact of non-belief” collapses too many distinct matters into one phrase: exposure to the message, comprehension, distortion, rejection, arrogance, doubt, excuse, opportunity, culpability, divine knowledge, warning, mercy, and final judgment. Islam does not teach that every human being is punished merely because a label called “non-belief” appears beside them. The Qur’an repeatedly ties accountability to truth reaching a person, rejection, moral response, and divine justice. “We never punish until We have sent a messenger” is not a decorative verse; it blocks the compressed premise. “Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity” blocks the caricature that divine judgment ignores circumstance. “Your Lord wrongs no one” blocks the move from human fear of punishment to the claim that the Judge is cruel.

The third failure is the hiddenness demand. The statement argues that if God knows what would convince every person, then God must provide whatever would produce that conviction. But this confuses guidance with coercive uptake. A person may be given signs, warnings, fitrah, reason, prophetic testimony, moral experience, and time, yet still demand a different kind of disclosure: not enough to know, but enough to remove the possibility of refusal. That demand changes the moral situation. It asks for a world in which the human will is overwhelmed until dissent becomes impossible or meaningless. Islam distinguishes between showing truth, guiding through signs, opening the heart, testing sincerity, and compelling outward submission. Divine wisdom is not measured by whether every person receives a custom-made experience that eliminates resistance.

The fourth failure is the proportionality assumption. The argument treats eternal punishment as if it were automatically disproportionate because disbelief is framed as a small cognitive mistake. But in the Islamic frame, kufr is not merely “I did not receive the right data point.” It can be a settled orientation of rejection, ingratitude, arrogance, denial, worship-displacement, and refusal of the One to whom the person owes existence, provision, truth, and return. The proportionality question therefore cannot be answered by shrinking the offense into “non-belief” and then comparing that minimized act to an infinite punishment. The real question is whether final punishment concerns a final, culpable, truth-resistant posture before the Creator after the relevant proofs, warnings, capacities, and excuses have been judged by Allah with perfect knowledge.

The fifth failure is attribute inversion. The statement says God is not kind or generous if He punishes. That assumes kindness and generosity exclude retribution. But even ordinary moral life does not work that way. A judge who never punishes oppression is not generous to victims. A ruler who treats betrayal, murder, corruption, and truth as morally equivalent is not merciful; he is unjust. Mercy does not mean the abolition of justice. Justice does not mean the absence of mercy. In Islam, Allah is al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm, al-ʿAdl in meaning though the named form is discussed in the tradition, al-Ḥakīm, al-Ghafūr, al-Qahhār, and Mālik Yawm al-Dīn. The attributes are not isolated slogans. They are held together under tawḥīd: the One who forgives, warns, guides, judges, delays, exposes, conceals, pardons, and punishes is not morally fragmented.

The sixth failure is worldview selectivity. If the statement is carried through a Satanist or TST frame, the analysis must distinguish the public worldview function from the person’s inner state. The identity label cannot be used as proof of motive, sincerity, culpability, or final standing. But the worldview claim can be evaluated. TST-style moral rhetoric often borrows moral absolutes while resisting transcendent moral authority. It can speak with strong confidence about cruelty, autonomy, justice, and dignity, yet it must still explain why those categories are binding rather than expressive preferences, social contracts, or rhetorical tools. If moral outrage against divine judgment is presented as objective, the source of that objectivity must be named. If it is not objective, it cannot dismantle worship.

The seventh failure is worship-worthiness confusion. The statement assumes that worship is owed only to a deity who satisfies the speaker’s prior moral expectations. Islam begins elsewhere: worship is owed to Allah because He is the only Creator, Sustainer, Lord, Giver of life, Giver of death, Revealer, Judge, and the only One worthy of ultimate love, fear, hope, surrender, and gratitude. The question is not whether God passes a humanly imported approval test. The question is whether the human being has recognized the Lord truthfully. If Allah is truly the Creator and Judge, then worship is not a consumer preference granted after moral inspection. It is the rightful response of the created being to the Real.

The eighth failure is treating rhetorical intensity as proof. “Cruel,” “inhumane,” “not kind,” “not generous,” and “not worthy” are serious predicates, but repetition does not establish them. The terms must be defined, sourced, and applied without contradiction. “Inhumane” is especially unstable when applied to God, because God is not measured by creaturely finitude. Humaneness can name a real moral concern about suffering and dignity, but it cannot mean that the Creator must be judged as though He were a limited human actor with partial knowledge, emotional instability, or institutional cruelty. Divine punishment is not a sadistic human prison system scaled upward. The argument needs that image to create force, but the image is not the doctrine.

What remains after these failures is not a sneer at the objector. The remaining truth is that the objection exposes a live moral burden: people are troubled by punishment, hiddenness, and accountability. That burden deserves careful daʿwah, not a shallow answer. But it must be answered in the right order. First, stabilize the moral source. Second, separate accountability from mere non-label. Third, distinguish guidance from coercion. Fourth, restore mercy and justice together. Fifth, evaluate the worldview carrier without pretending to know the person’s inner state. Sixth, call the hearer back to tawḥīd, fiṭrah, and sound reason rather than letting outrage become the final judge.

### Restored criterion/orientation

The restored criterion is not “Would I worship a deity whose judgment initially offends me?” The restored criterion is: Who is Allah, what has He revealed about Himself, what does sound reason recognize under revelation, and what does the human being owe to the One who created, sustains, warns, forgives, and judges?

On tawḥīd, Allah is not one moral actor inside a shared universe, competing with human beings under a neutral law above Him. Allah is the source of existence, truth, moral reality, command, mercy, justice, and return. That does not mean “might makes right.” It means the opposite: right is not floating above Allah, nor invented by human preference, nor reducible to power. The Real, the True, the Wise, the Just, and the Merciful are not external constraints imposed on Allah; they are known through His names, acts, revelation, and the order He creates. A moral objection that forgets this will misdescribe both God and morality.

On fiṭrah, the human being is not wrong to feel that cruelty is evil. The instinct that oppression is hateful, that arbitrary torture is monstrous, that mercy matters, and that worship must not be given to evil is not something daʿwah should crush. Those recognitions are part of the reason the objection has force. But fiṭrah must be purified from inversion. It can be bent into self-sovereignty, where “I recognize cruelty” becomes “I define justice over God.” It can be narrowed into sentimentalism, where mercy is imagined as the cancellation of all consequence. It can be manipulated by vivid images, where “lake of fire” is heard through human sadism rather than divine judgment. Restored fiṭrah returns the moral sense to its Lord.

On ʿaql ṣarīḥ, the argument must be asked to account for its own premises. If objective cruelty exists, what grounds it? If moral truth binds all persons, why? If the Creator exists and reveals, why should creaturely moral discomfort override the Creator’s own account of justice and mercy? If punishment is rejected absolutely, what happens to moral seriousness? If punishment is rejected only in this case, what exact premise makes it unjust: duration, intensity, epistemic access, culpability, or the nature of the offense? Sound reason does not permit the argument to hide inside intensity. It asks for definitions, dependencies, and warrant.

On accountability, the restored criterion separates cases Allah has separated. There is a difference between never hearing the message, hearing a distorted version, misunderstanding through weakness, wrestling with sincere doubt, refusing out of pride, mocking truth, exploiting sin as an identity shield, and knowingly rejecting the Lord. Allah knows these distinctions perfectly. Human beings do not. Therefore daʿwah should not pronounce individual final outcomes. It should clarify the principle: Allah’s judgment is not ignorant, arbitrary, forgetful, or unjust. He knows what reached a person, what they understood, what they resisted, what they sought, what they loved, what they hid, what they were capable of, and what excuses apply.

On hiddenness, the restored criterion asks what kind of conviction is being demanded. If the demand is for adequate signs, revelation, reason, moral experience, prophetic witness, and invitation, Islam affirms that Allah guides through signs and does not wrong people. If the demand is for irresistible private proof that guarantees every person’s acceptance, then the demand is no longer simply for justice. It is a demand to redesign the test so that the distinction between humility and defiance, trust and arrogance, surrender and self-rule, becomes morally flattened. A world with no possibility of refusal is not the same as a world with just guidance.

On punishment, the restored criterion refuses both denial and caricature. It does not deny Hell because modern sentiment dislikes it. It also does not speak about Hell with cruelty, triumphalism, or speculative confidence about named individuals. Hell is a revealed warning, a display of divine justice, and a terror that should humble the speaker before it is used against anyone else. The Qur’an’s warnings are paired with calls to repent, descriptions of mercy, reminders of delayed punishment, and repeated openings for return. A daʿwah answer must not make punishment sound like the center of Islam detached from mercy. Nor may it erase punishment to make Islam acceptable to a secular moral tribunal.

On mercy, the restored criterion is that Allah’s mercy is not sentimental permissiveness. Mercy includes guidance before judgment, messengers before punishment, warning before consequence, repentance before death, forgiveness of vast sin, concealment of faults, acceptance of return, and the multiplication of good. Mercy also includes justice for the wronged and the refusal to let evil become equal to good. A person who demands mercy for the rejecter must also ask about mercy for victims, mercy for the truthful, mercy for those who sacrificed under obedience, and mercy for the order of reality itself.

On worship-worthiness, the restored criterion is that worship belongs to Allah because He is Allah. The human being may ask, struggle, seek, and need clarification. But the posture must move from tribunal to servanthood. The question “Would I worship?” should be transformed into “Have I understood whom worship is due to?” If the One being discussed is the Creator of the heavens and earth, the giver of every breath, the source of moral truth, the revealer of guidance, and the Judge who knows every hidden thing, then refusal to worship is not moral nobility. It is a failure to locate oneself in reality.

On sources, the restored orientation gives the Qur’an priority, then the Sunnah, then disciplined scholarly explanation, then comparative worldview analysis. The Qur’an establishes that Allah does not wrong anyone, that punishment is not sent without messenger-warning, that souls are not burdened beyond capacity, that mercy is vast, that repentance is open, that rejection can be culpable, and that final judgment belongs to Allah. The Sunnah deepens the picture with Allah’s mercy, the seriousness of intention, the danger of arrogance, and the reality of accountability. Worldview sources may be used to analyze TST moral and political claims, but they do not outrank revelation when defining divine justice.

A concise daʿwah formulation would therefore be:

Your objection rightly senses that arbitrary cruelty would make worship morally impossible. Islam agrees that Allah is not cruel, not ignorant, not unjust, and not arbitrary. But your conclusion depends on compressing all disbelief into a simple innocent non-belief, treating guidance as if justice requires guaranteed uptake, and placing an ungrounded moral tribunal over God. Islam separates those issues. Allah judges only with perfect knowledge, does not punish without rightful proof and accountability, knows every excuse, opens repentance, and joins mercy with justice. The real question is not whether Allah passes a human approval test, but whether your moral outrage has been returned to its source: the One Creator who gave you the very capacity to recognize injustice.

Another concise formulation would be:

If by “non-belief” you mean a person who never truly received or understood the truth, Islam does not let us casually assign that person to Hell. If you mean a culpable rejection of the Creator after truth has been made clear, then the issue is no longer a small intellectual mistake. It is rebellion against the One to whom worship, gratitude, and surrender are due. Your argument becomes powerful only by switching between those two meanings.

And a firmer formulation would be:

You are using moral categories that require grounding: cruelty, kindness, generosity, justice, worthiness. Islam can ground them in Allah, revelation, fiṭrah, and sound reason. A worldview that rejects divine authority must explain why those categories bind anyone beyond preference, culture, or political usefulness. Until that source is named, the objection borrows moral capital from the very kind of reality it is trying to judge.

The restored endpoint is not merely apologetic victory. It is a call to reorient the heart and reason: let the fear of injustice return to the Just, let the longing for mercy return to the Merciful, let the demand for truth return to the True, and let the horror of Hell become a reason to seek guidance rather than a reason to enthrone the self as judge over revelation. Tawḥīd restores the order: Allah is not weighed by the servant; the servant is weighed by Allah, with a justice and mercy no servant can exceed.

### Scoped boundary

This answer does not claim to know Richard-Lael Lillard’s inner state, sincerity, final accountability, private motives, or fate. The named identity and TST association are treated as public worldview-carrier context only. They may help identify the kinds of moral authority, autonomy, anti-theistic, and symbolic-inversion claims that often travel with the statement, but they do not prove what is in the person’s heart. The daʿwah boundary is to answer the assertion, not to pretend to own the unseen.
