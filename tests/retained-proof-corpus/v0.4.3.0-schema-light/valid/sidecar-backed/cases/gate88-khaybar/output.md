Initial orientation:

The Qur'anic promise in 5:67 is not a claim that no human being could ever touch, wound, distress, poison, or physically affect the Prophet ﷺ in any respect. The verse is tied to conveying the message: "Allah will protect you from the people." The protection relevant to the verse is protection from the people preventing the Messenger ﷺ from completing and delivering the revelation entrusted to him. That reading is also the one required by the history itself: the Prophet ﷺ was opposed, mocked, struck, besieged, wounded, and plotted against, yet none of those human efforts stopped the completion of the message.

So the scope of protection must be named before formalization. If P(x) means "Allah prevents any bodily effect from any human action at all," then the definition is already false against the agreed prophetic biography. If P(x) means "Allah prevents the people from overpowering the mission and stopping the Messenger ﷺ from conveying what he was commanded to convey," then the Khaybar poisoning does not contradict it. The Prophet ﷺ did not die at Khaybar. The mission continued. Revelation continued. The religion was completed. The death occurred years later after completion, not as a successful t1 termination of the mission.

The hadith wording itself also resists the objection's compression. In the report from Anas, the woman says she wanted to kill him, and the Prophet ﷺ says that Allah would not have enabled her to do that. The immediate sense is not "no poison entered his body" or "no pain could ever remain." It is that her intended killing plot would not be enabled to accomplish its intended object. The historical result matches that: she did not kill him at Khaybar; Allah preserved him, and the public mission continued.

The later report from A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, says that during the illness in which he died, the Prophet ﷺ still felt the effect of what he had eaten at Khaybar, and he described the severity of that final pain. This can support his martyrdom and the lasting trace of the poison without proving that the Jewish woman's plot successfully killed him at t1. A delayed bodily effect is not identical to a mission-stopping fatal success at the time of the plot. The argument needs that identity, but the sources do not grant it.

The lexical point about al-wateen and al-abhar is also relevant, but it is not the whole refutation. Even if one discusses overlap, anatomical usage, or dictionary ranges, the logic tree still fails earlier because it smuggles a conclusion into H(t1). The Qur'anic threat in 69:44-46 concerns a hypothetical false attribution to Allah: if the Messenger had fabricated speech against Allah, Allah Himself would seize him and cut the life-vessel. That is a divine judgment scenario, not a prediction that any later mention of pain near a major vessel would prove false prophethood. The opponent has to equate separate contexts: divine punishment for fabrication, enemy poisoning at Khaybar, later illness, and martyrdom. Those are not interchangeable merely because English translations may use similar anatomical language.

The clean issue is this: the formal argument does not derive a contradiction from Islam's premises. It derives a contradiction from a replacement premise.

The replacement premise says:

At t1, while the message was ongoing, a human plot successfully inflicted fatal bodily harm.

But the reports establish a different set of propositions:

At t1, a human enemy introduced poison and intended to kill the Prophet ﷺ.
At t1, Allah did not enable that plot to kill him or stop his mission.
After t1, the Prophet ﷺ continued to live and deliver the message.
At t2, after completion of the message, he died during his final illness while still feeling the effect of the Khaybar poison.
Muslim scholars may describe that as martyrdom without making the enemy's t1 plot victorious over Allah's protection.

Those propositions are coherent together. The contradiction only appears when "poison introduced" becomes "fatal harm successfully inflicted at t1," and when "protection from the people" becomes "absolute immunity from all bodily effects." Both moves are illicit.

A concise answer to the objection is therefore: the woman at Khaybar attempted to kill the Prophet, but Allah did not allow her attempt to accomplish its intended mission-ending result. The reports of later pain do not change the earlier outcome into success. They show that created causes can leave bodily traces, while Allah's promise still governs the completion of revelation and the preservation of the Messenger until he delivers what he was commanded to deliver.

The argument fails because it treats “poison affected him” as identical to “the enemies overpowered Allah's protection.” Those are not the same proposition. Poison can affect a body without invalidating prophethood, without implying fabrication, and without proving that the divine promise in Qur'an 5:67 failed. The relevant denied claim is not bodily vulnerability; it is human control over the success or failure of Allah's Messenger in conveying Allah's message.

This also keeps the Qur'anic passages from being made to fight each other. Qur'an 5:67 speaks about Allah preserving His Messenger against the people in the duty of conveying revelation. Qur'an 69:44-46 speaks about the impossible case of the Messenger fabricating against Allah and being seized by Allah. The Khaybar reports speak about a human poisoning attempt and later bodily suffering. When each text is read according to its condition, subject, and time, there is no cross-text contradiction.

The hostile reading depends on moving predicates across boundaries. It takes a human attempt and calls it successful fatal harm. It takes later pain and calls it proof of an earlier contradiction. It takes a counterfactual divine punishment for fabrication and tries to apply it to an affirmed truthful Messenger. It takes protection for the mission and inflates it into absolute immunity from created harm. None of these moves is required by the sources, and each move carries the conclusion inside it.

A faithful reading can affirm all of the relevant data at once:

The Prophet was human and could be harmed.
The Khaybar poisoning was a real attempt.
Allah did not allow that attempt to kill him in the intended way.
The Prophet continued after Khaybar.
The message was conveyed and completed.
His final illness and death occurred under Allah's decree.
The Qur'anic promise was not defeated by the people.

That set is coherent. It does not require denial of the hadith, denial of bodily suffering, or denial of martyrdom language. It only requires refusing the objector's inflated definition of protection and refusing the premature labeling of Khaybar as a successful mission-ending event.

The burden remains on the objector. To produce the alleged contradiction, he must show more than poison, pain, or later death. He must show that the human plot stopped the Messenger before he conveyed the revelation Allah commanded him to convey. The historical sequence does not provide that. It provides the opposite sequence: attempted killing, survival, continued prophetic activity, completion of the message, and later death.

So the restored order is not evasive. It is the order demanded by the evidence. Attempt comes before consequence. Consequence is not automatically success. Later death is not automatically t1 fatality. Bodily harm is not automatically failure of divine protection. Related anatomical wording is not automatically activation of a Qur'anic punishment passage whose stated condition is fabrication.

The objection also misses the theological center. Allah's decree is not judged by whether an enemy can initiate a cause. It is judged by whether Allah's promised outcome stands. The enemies of the Prophet plotted, harmed, fought, and tried to extinguish the message. They did not prevent its delivery. That is exactly the point of the protection claim. Human hostility appears inside the story as a defeated pressure, not as a rival sovereignty over Allah's command.

The conclusion is therefore stable: Qur'an 5:67 and the Khaybar reports can be held together without contradiction. The reports do not say that the Prophet was killed at Khaybar before completing his mission. They say an attempt occurred, harm followed, and Allah did not enable the intended killing. The objection becomes persuasive only by changing the meanings of protection, fatality, success, and punishment. Once those meanings are restored to their proper scope, the alleged contradiction dissolves.
Closing Formulation

The refutation closes at the level of the argument's own load-bearing claim: the alleged contradiction is not produced by the Qur'an, the hadith reports, or Arabic lexical evidence. It is produced by a compressed predicate system that changes the meaning of the sources before the logic tree begins.

The proposed tree says that in 628 CE the message was still ongoing and a human plot successfully inflicted fatal bodily harm. From that imported premise it derives a contradiction with divine protection. But that is exactly where the argument fails. The reports do not state that the woman killed the Prophet at Khaybar in 628 CE. They state that he ate from the poisoned meat, was protected from being killed by that plot at that time, lived afterward, completed the message, and later, in his final illness, felt the lasting pain of that earlier poisoning while Allah decreed his death. The formal contradiction only appears after "poisoned exposure with lasting harm" is silently treated as "successful fatal human killing while protection was active."

Surah 5:67 is not a promise that no hostile human being will ever touch, injure, grieve, wound, poison, or physically affect the Prophet. It is a protection promise tied to the delivery and completion of the message: "Allah will protect you from the people." The historical record fits that meaning. The Prophet was not prevented from human opposition, injury, siege, slander, battle wounds, grief, illness, or ordinary embodied suffering. He was protected from being stopped, killed, silenced, or overcome before the message was conveyed. Khaybar therefore cannot be used as a contradiction unless the critic first broadens the verse into a total-immunity thesis that Islam itself does not teach.

Nor does the hadith wording force the critic's equation. The report of the woman says she wanted to kill him, and he answered that Allah would not give her power to do that. That reply is direct evidence against the premise that her plot successfully killed him at Khaybar. The later report from his final illness says that he still felt the effect of the food from Khaybar and now felt as if his aorta were being cut from that poison. That is not the same proposition as "the human plot killed him in 628." It is a report of lingering bodily effect under Allah's decree after the mission had been completed.

The lexical point also damages the attempted proof rather than supporting it. The Qur'anic threat in 69:44-46 uses al-wateen in the context of Allah's own hypothetical punishment if a messenger were to fabricate revelation. The hadith wording uses al-abhar in the Prophet's description of his final pain. Even where the terms overlap in some anatomical usage, the argument has not earned identity of context, identity of agent, identity of judgment, or identity of event. A divine threat concerning fabricated revelation is not the same proposition as a human poisoning attempt whose immediate killing purpose failed and whose residual effect was later felt by Allah's Messenger.

### Established failure

The first established failure is predicate inflation. The symbol H is defined as "a human plot successfully inflicting fatal bodily harm." That definition is then loaded into t1, the year of Khaybar. But the historical material supplied in the prompt does not establish H(t1). It establishes attempted killing, poisoned exposure, survival, continued mission, and later illness. The fatal outcome occurs at the end of the Prophet's earthly life, not at the moment when the woman attempted to kill him. If H means "successful fatal harm at t1," the evidence does not prove it. If H is weakened to "some bodily harm from a human plot," then Premise 2 no longer follows from Surah 5:67.

That means the proof equivocates. It needs H to be strong enough to contradict protection, but the source evidence only supports a weaker claim: a human enemy introduced poison, Allah did not allow her to kill him then, and the poison left an effect that he later felt. The contradiction is therefore not between Islam and history. It is between the critic's definition of H and the actual reports.

The second established failure is scope substitution. The argument treats P as if it means absolute protection from all bodily harm. But the Qur'anic protection in 5:67 is tied to conveying what was revealed. The verse addresses the Messenger's duty to proclaim and Allah's protection from the people in that mission-bearing context. It does not erase the Prophet's humanity, mortality, pain, illness, battle wounds, or eventual death. The Prophet's life itself refutes that exaggerated reading: he faced injury at Uhud, persecution in Makkah, military danger, grief, hunger, and illness. None of that contradicted divine protection because the protection was not a claim of bodily invulnerability.

The third established failure is time collapse. The argument binds t1 to both ongoing mission and successful fatal harm. But the narrative distinguishes the time of poisoning from the time of death. At t1, the attempt did not achieve its killing purpose. After t1, the Prophet continued to teach, judge, lead, receive and convey revelation, perform the Farewell Pilgrimage, and complete the public delivery of the religion. At t2, he died under Allah's decree after the message had been completed. The logic tree erases this interval, then uses the erased interval as proof of contradiction.

The fourth established failure is agent confusion. Surah 69:44-46 describes what Allah would do if the Messenger had fabricated speech against Him. The alleged parallel tries to move from that divine punitive scenario to a human poisoning report. But the agents are different, the moral condition is different, and the theological function is different. In the Qur'anic passage, the cutting of the vital vessel is a divine sign of impossible prophetic fabrication being punished by Allah. In the hadith report, the Prophet speaks of pain connected to a past poisoning attempt while dying as Allah's Messenger after completing his mission. The argument makes an analogy where it needs identity.

The fifth established failure is lexical overreach. The distinction between al-wateen and al-abhar is relevant because the critic's case often depends on claiming a neat verbal match between Qur'anic threat and hadith death report. The prompt itself notes that al-abhar is a broader bodily term in the cited lexical discussion, and that al-wateen may be identified as a specific part within that broader description. Even if a translator renders both with "aorta" in English, English glosses do not collapse Arabic usage, source context, and theological predication into one proof. Translation overlap is not a contradiction engine.

The sixth established failure is misuse of formalization. Formal logic is only as sound as the meanings fed into it. The tree's natural deduction is valid in a narrow mechanical sense if its premises are granted. But the disputed premise is precisely the one that the sources do not grant. A valid derivation from an imported false premise is not a refutation. The formal diagram creates a clean contradiction by first cleaning away the distinctions that matter: attempt versus success, exposure versus fatal killing, immediate death versus later death, mission-protection versus total bodily immunity, divine punishment versus human plot, and Arabic source wording versus English gloss.

The seventh established failure is contradiction by stipulation. Premise 3 says M(t1) and H(t1). Premise 1 and Premise 2 then yield not-H(t1). The "explosion" therefore does not expose Islam as contradictory. It exposes that the critic has stipulated a premise that conflicts with the very protection claim under discussion. The rational question is whether H(t1) is actually established. It is not. The woman's intention was to kill. The Prophet's response denies that she was enabled to do so. The later pain does not turn the failed immediate plot into a successful fatal killing at t1.

The eighth established failure is source-order inversion. A sound reading begins with the Qur'anic scope, then the authenticated reports, then the Arabic wording, then historical sequencing, then any formal abstraction. The argument reverses this order. It builds symbols first, forces the sources into them, and then announces the resulting symbolic crash as if it were a discovery. That is not ʿaql ṣarīḥ; it is a brittle abstraction placed above its own evidence.

The ninth established failure is treating martyrdom as contradiction. Ibn al-Qayyim's statement that the Prophet died as a martyr does not mean the woman's plot overpowered divine protection before the message was complete. In Islamic terms, martyrdom can name an honor Allah grants through the circumstances of death. It does not imply that Allah failed to protect His Messenger from being prevented from delivering revelation. The Prophet's death after completion of the message and after Allah's religion had been conveyed is not a defeat of the promise; it is within Allah's decree over every human life.

The tenth established failure is ignoring the Prophet's own reported answer. When the woman said she wanted to kill him, he said Allah would not have enabled her to do that. The critic's premise says she did do that. A reading that makes the hadith contradict its own direct statement has not handled the report faithfully. The more coherent reading is simple: she intended murder, Allah blocked the intended fatal result at the time, and the remaining bodily effect became part of the Prophet's later illness and martyrdom.

### Restored criterion/orientation

The restored criterion is: does the evidence show that a human plot stopped the Prophet from conveying the message or killed him before the protected mission was complete? The answer is no.

A sound formulation would distinguish the claims this way. M(t) should refer to the message-bearing mission still requiring public conveyance. P(t) should refer to Allah's protection from people overpowering or preventing that mission. H(t) should refer only to a successful human killing that terminates the mission before completion. Under those definitions, the Khaybar event does not yield H(t1). It yields attempted-H(t1), poisoned-exposure(t1), survival-after(t1), and completed-message-before-death(t2). The contradiction disappears because the premise that created it was never established.

The correct orientation is also tawhid-centered. Allah is the protector, decreer, giver of life, giver of death, and Lord over causes. Human beings may plot, administer poison, swing swords, spread slander, or intend harm, but their causes do not become independent powers against Allah. The woman at Khaybar intended to kill. Allah did not give her that power in the mission-destroying sense. The Prophet lived until Allah completed the religion through him. When death came, it came by Allah's decree, not because a human plot escaped divine lordship.

This preserves fitrah because the reading does not ask the heart and reason to pretend that poison is harmless or that prophets are disembodied. The Prophet was fully human. He could suffer, bleed, become ill, feel pain, and die. His truthfulness is not proven by denying his embodied humanity. It is shown in the completion of his mission, the preservation of revelation, the defeat of attempts to silence him before conveyance, and the coherence of his reports when read with their proper scope.

It also preserves sound reason, ʿaql ṣarīḥ, because it refuses an invalid identity claim. Reason does not permit a critic to take "attempted poisoning with lasting effect," rename it "successful fatal human harm at the time of attempt," and then derive a contradiction from that renaming. Reason requires stable predicates. It requires that the same term carry the same meaning across the proof. It requires that the time index be respected. It requires that a text about divine punishment for fabricated revelation not be casually merged with a report about human poisoning. It requires that Arabic terms and contexts be handled before an English gloss is made to bear the whole argument.

The restored reading therefore lands as follows: Surah 5:67 promises protection from the people in relation to the Messenger's obligation to convey revelation. The Khaybar report shows an attempted murder that failed to kill him when the mission was ongoing. The later illness report shows lingering pain connected to that poisoning at the time Allah decreed his death. Surah 69:44-46 speaks of Allah's hypothetical punishment if the Messenger had forged revelation, not a human plot at Khaybar. The lexical distinction between al-wateen and al-abhar further weakens the claimed verbal match. The logic tree does not refute Islam; it refutes a caricature predicate.

A better formal sketch would look like this in prose. If the message remains to be conveyed, Allah protects the Messenger from human beings preventing that conveyance. At Khaybar, a human enemy attempted to kill him through poison. Allah did not allow that attempt to terminate his life or mission at that time. The Messenger continued and the message was completed. Later, after completion, he died while feeling the effect of the earlier poison, and that can be understood as martyrdom under Allah's decree. No contradiction follows.

The restored criterion also corrects the handling of "lethal dose." Even if the poison was intrinsically lethal or intended as lethal, that does not mean it successfully killed him at t1. A cause may be lethal by nature and still not achieve the intended death because Allah prevents, delays, redirects, or decrees the outcome otherwise. The critic's argument needs "lethal" to mean "actually fatal at the protected time." The reports do not grant that. They present a failed killing attempt whose traces remained.

The restored orientation finally returns the issue to prophetic truth rather than polemical cleverness. The Qur'an does not present the Prophet as safe from all pain. It presents him as commanded to convey and protected in that conveyance. The hadith does not present the woman as victorious over Allah's promise. It presents her intention as exposed and her killing purpose as denied. The Prophet's later death does not prove revelation was false. It proves that Allah's Messenger, like all human messengers before him, completed his appointed task and then returned to his Lord.

This is why the fitrah and sound reason converge here. Fitrah recognizes the difference between a failed assassination attempt and a mission-ending defeat. Sound reason recognizes the difference between a valid deduction and a true premise. Tawhid recognizes that causes are not independent of Allah. The Qur'an, hadith, Arabic wording, and historical sequence all remain coherent once those distinctions are restored.

### Scoped boundary

This closure does not claim that every possible discussion about Khaybar, medical causality, Arabic anatomy terms, or martyrdom terminology has been exhausted. It closes the specific contradiction claim as presented: the claim that Surah 5:67, the Khaybar poisoning reports, and the later illness report generate a formal logical contradiction.

The boundary is narrow but sufficient. The argument fails because H(t1) is not established in the sense required by the proof. It fails because P(t1) is inflated beyond the verse's mission-protection scope. It fails because t1 and t2 are collapsed. It fails because divine punitive language in Surah 69 is confused with a human poisoning report. It fails because Arabic lexical and contextual distinctions are flattened under an English gloss. It fails because the formal tree depends on imported definitions rather than source-preserving predicates.

What remains open, if someone wants to pursue it honestly, is a more careful historical and lexical study: the precise isnad and matn variants of the Khaybar reports, the range of al-abhar and al-wateen in Arabic lexica, the medical language of "aorta" in translation, and the theological category of martyrdom. But none of those reopen the presented contradiction unless they can establish the missing proposition: that a human plot successfully killed the Prophet before the message was completed while Allah's promise of mission-protection was still unmet. The quoted material does not establish that proposition.

The endpoint is therefore stable: the logic tree's branch closes only inside its own artificial predicate set. It does not close against the Islamic sources. The Qur'anic promise stands as protection over conveyance. The hadith reports stand as attempted poisoning, survival, later pain, and death by Allah's decree after completion. The lexical evidence prevents a careless one-word equivalence from carrying the proof. The formal derivation loses force because its decisive premise is not source-owned.

So the final answer to the refutation target is: no contradiction has been shown. The alleged contradiction is manufactured by equivocation over protection, fatal harm, time, agency, and wording. A fitrah-aligned and ʿaql-governed reading keeps Allah's lordship over causes, the Prophet's human embodiment, the completion of revelation, and the historical reports together without forcing any of them to deny the others.
